# The slide types, and the look they build

Every slide in a She Sharp deck is one of the types below. There is no free-form
slide and no "just put a text box here" — which is why the decks look like one
thing rather than eighteen things.

Pick the layout that matches the **shape** of what you are saying, not the
subject. Five things you want the room to weigh up are `themes`, whether they
are hackathon challenges, workshop tracks or panel topics.

---

## What the deck is made of

**Two things, and the line between them is fixed.**

The **organisational sequence** — the karakia, who we are, the team, the impact
figures, the sponsor wall, the thanks, the closing — is built out of She Sharp's
own photographs, under the brand purple, in every deck, forever. That is the
doctrine set out in the rest of this section, and no event may restyle it.

**Everything from the chapter card onwards is the event's**, and it wears that
event's own skin — a surface, palette, geometry, motion tempo and type
personality designed from the event's poster. See `references/skins.md`. A Les
Mills panel evening and a two-day AI hackathon should not look like the same
deck, and until August 2026 they did.

So read what follows as the rule for the house half, and as the *reason the
house half exists* — the archive is what the event's own look is set against.

### Why the archive, for the house half

This half is not built out of design. It is built out of **She Sharp's own
photographs**, and the reason matters enough to say plainly.

Taken one at a time, the archive is not good. It is group shots in meeting rooms
under fluorescent tubes, no space around anybody, and a white balance that
wanders across four stops from one event to the next. Judged as photographs,
most of them would not survive a portfolio review.

Judged as a record, they are the entire story. Twelve years of the same ritual —
the same rooms, the same chairs pushed back, the same people getting older in
the background — is a thing no stock library and no generated image can produce,
and no other organisation in the country has it. So the deck stops apologising
for the archive and starts using it as **mass**: many photographs at once,
filling a slide, read as one surface rather than as individual pictures.

Two consequences you have to hold at the same time:

- **A single photograph used as a subject stays in full colour.** The winning
  team, the speaker mid-sentence, the room from the back — when the audience is
  meant to look *at* the picture, it is a picture, and it keeps its own colours.
- **Anything used en masse is purple.** The moment photographs are being used as
  texture rather than as subjects, they take a purple duotone — the whole group
  reduced to one colour so that forty different white balances stop fighting
  each other and start reading as one field.

The second rule is what makes the first rule work. If everything were full
colour, nothing would be; the duotone is what buys a single photograph the right
to be looked at.

**Neither of these is a setting.** The layout decides: put photographs on a
slide as a wall, a band or a mosaic and they come out purple; use one as the
subject of a slide and it keeps its colours. There is no switch, and there
should not be one — the grade is the only thing making twelve years of
photography shot in twelve different lighting conditions read as one deck, so
turning it off for one slide would not customise the deck, it would break it. If
an author asks, that is the answer: *"the purple is what holds them together;
the photo you want in full colour wants to be its own slide."*

**Where words go when there is a wall behind them.** Not on top of a dimmed
photograph — the deck does not fade the wall down and print over it. Type sits
on a solid panel *cut into* the wall, with the photographs continuing at full
strength on either side. You do not build that yourself, but knowing it is the
house move explains why a slide has a hard-edged block of colour in it and why
you should not go looking for a way to put a caption directly over the tiles.

**Practical effect on you:** when you choose an image for a slide, decide which
of the two things it is. "Here is the winner" is a subject. "Here is what twelve
years looks like" is mass. A slide that cannot answer the question is usually a
slide with one photograph on it for no reason.

And when it is mass, **you do not choose the photographs** — they come from a
pool of 130 archive images spanning 2014 to 2026, spread deliberately across the
years so a wall is never all one shoot. `references/assets.md` covers what to
say when an author asks for a particular photo to be in it.

## Words filled with a photograph

Wherever the deck fills letterforms with imagery, one constraint applies and it
is not negotiable: **display size only, on a short word or a numeral.** The
prototype's giant `01` is the case the treatment was designed for.

The same treatment applied to the word `Aotearoa` was unreadable. Nine letters
at that weight leave each one holding a sliver of picture, and a reader gets
neither the word nor the photograph. If a word is longer than about six letters,
set it in a colour and put the photograph behind it instead.

## The whole set at a glance

| Type | What it looks like | Holds | Reach for it when |
|---|---|---|---|
| `title` | Big headline on a dark slide, a few facts, a row of partner logos | 3 facts | Opening or closing the deck |
| `karakia` | Te reo on one side, English on the other, dark and quiet | Both languages, required | Opening and closing the event |
| `section` | A single chapter title, dark, optional photo behind | 1 title | Marking a new part of the day |
| `bullets` | Title and a short list | 5 bullets, 10 words each | A handful of separate points |
| `agenda` | Times down the left, short labels beside them | 14 rows | The run sheet |
| `people` | A grid of faces with names and job titles | Any number, density controls the size | Introducing the team, speakers, judges |
| `photo` | One photograph, full bleed, optional words over it | 1 image | Letting a moment land |
| `photo-grid` | Three to five photos as a composition | 3–5 images | Showing what an event feels like |
| `stats` | Three or four big numbers | 4 figures | Impact, scale, results |
| `logos` | Rows of logos on white chips, optionally grouped by tier | Any number, grouped | Sponsors and partners |
| `themes` | Cards, each a short title and one line | 6 cards | Options the room has to choose between |
| `criteria` | Named rows with a short description and a weight | 5 rows | How something is judged or assessed |
| `prizes` | Amounts with what they are for | 4 rows | The prize pool |
| `break` | Big countdown clock, dark | 1–120 minutes | Any break |
| `qr-cta` | One code, large, with up to three supporting lines | 1 code, 3 points | One thing you want the room to do now |
| `contact` | Social handles plus two or three codes | 3 codes | How to find She Sharp |
| `upcoming` | Two or three future events with dates | 3 events | The end, before the closing |
| `thanks` | Grouped logos plus names | Any number | Thanking partners and people |

Dark slides: `title`, `karakia`, `section`, `break`. Everything else is light,
matching the website. You can override it per slide, but the default is the
rhythm the deck was designed around — dark for the moments that want the room to
go quiet, light for everything informational.

## The two kinds of slide, for rhythm

The checker sorts every type into one of two piles, and then complains about
long runs of either. This is the table to look at when it does.

| | Types | What they are for |
|---|---|---|
| **Full-frame** | `title`, `section`, `karakia`, `break`, `photo`, `prizes` | Breathing. The screen fills, there is almost nothing to read, and the room looks up |
| **Information** | everything else | Working. The room reads, or follows along while someone talks |

The limits: **no more than 2 full-frame in a row**, **no more than 4 information
in a row**, **no more than 4 of the same tone in a row**, **at least a quarter of
a 12-slide deck dark**, and **at least 8 different layouts in a deck of 10 or
more**.

Two ways to read that:

- **Three statement slides back to back cancel each other out.** A chapter
  divider straight after a full-bleed photograph straight after a karakia is
  three moments asking for the same silence, and the third one gets a room
  waiting for the actual content.
- **Five information slides in a row is where the room goes.** `prizes` counts
  as full-frame on purpose — it is a reveal, not a table — which is why it can
  break a long working stretch on its own.

`references/copy-rules.md` has what to do when a run is flagged. The short
version: add a beat in the middle of the run, never reorder the day.

## Which layout should I use for this?

| What you have | Use |
|---|---|
| A run sheet — times and what happens | `agenda` |
| Five challenge statements teams pick from | `themes` |
| A scoring rubric | `criteria` |
| "$5,000 for the winning team, $1,000 for runner-up" | `prizes` |
| Three speakers with headshots | `people` |
| Three things people need to know about the venue | `bullets` |
| A photo of last year's room | `photo` |
| Five photos of last year | `photo-grid` |
| "3000+ members, 94 events, 50 sponsors" | `stats` |
| Nine sponsor logos in two tiers | `logos` |
| "Scan this to give us feedback" | `qr-cta` |
| "Here's where to find us" with a couple of codes | `contact` |
| "We're back in September, and again in November" | `upcoming` |
| The end of a chapter, before the next one starts | `section` |
| Fifteen minutes for coffee | `break` |

Two rules of thumb worth applying before you pick anything:

- **Bullets are the default, and the default is usually wrong.** Almost every
  list of five items is really three themes, or four criteria, or a stats slide.
  The linter blocks a third bullet slide in a row for exactly this reason.
- **If two layouts both fit, pick the one with fewer words on screen.**

## Each type, in a bit more detail

**All of them carry a kicker** — the short line above the title, five or six
words, different on every slide and never a restatement of the title or the
section label. It is a required part of writing a slide, not a per-layout
feature. `references/copy-rules.md` has the rule and the examples.

### `title`

The event's name at full size on a dark slide, with up to three short facts
under it — a date, a venue, "free entry" — and a row of partner logos along the
bottom. Every deck opens with one; the closing "thank you for coming" version is
the same layout in its end variant.

> *Aotearoa AI Hackathon Festival 2026 · 7–8 August · AUT City Campus*

### `karakia`

A dark, quiet slide with the te reo on one side and the English translation on
the other. Both are required — the linter rejects one without the other, because
half a karakia on a screen is worse than none.

The opening (tīmatanga) and closing (whakamutunga) are fixed and come free with
every deck. You do not author these.

**It can take a background, and it should.** The archive has nothing that suits
a karakia — every photograph in it is indoors under artificial light — so there
are three generated whenua plates for this: a greenstone sea, an unfurling
frond, harakeke at dusk. `references/assets.md` explains what they are, what
they may never depict, and why.

### `section`

One chapter title on a dark slide, optionally with a chapter number and a photo
behind it. It is a breath, not information. Use it when the day turns a corner —
after the welcome, before the workshop, before judging.

> *02 — The challenge*

It is also the cheapest fix in the deck. Almost every time the checker complains
about a long run of information slides, a divider was missing from the middle of
it anyway — the day had already turned a corner and the deck had not noticed.

Behind it, use either a real photograph from the event or one of the three
abstract light plates.

The chapter number is set enormous, in outline, and it is the one thing on the
slide carrying where you are in the running order. It is also the only kind of
word that survives being treated that way — two characters, no letterforms to
lose. Give it `"01"`, not `"Chapter one"`.

### `bullets`

A title, optionally one sentence underneath, and up to five short lines. One or
two columns. Can carry a supporting image on the trailing edge, which is a good
way to stop a bullet slide feeling like a bullet slide.

Health & safety is the archetype: three things, six words each, and everyone in
the room needs all three.

### `agenda`

The run sheet. A clock time on the left, a very short label on the right, up to
fourteen rows. One row can be emphasised in the accent colour — use it for the
"you are here" moment, the thing the room actually came for.

A two-day event gets one slide per day rather than one slide with twenty-eight
rows.

> *5:45pm — Welcome and karakia*

### `people`

Faces in a grid with names and job titles. Three densities: large shows four
across with job titles, medium six across, small eight across with names only.
A missing headshot falls back to an initials tile rather than a gap.

Job titles are six words maximum. Nobody's biography goes here.

### `photo`

One photograph filling the slide, with an optional title and one line over it.
The overlay can be off, a flat scrim, or a gradient — use a scrim or gradient
whenever there is text, or the text disappears into the picture.

Reach for it when the thing you want is a feeling, not a fact: the room last
year, the winning team, the wall of sticky notes.

This is the **subject** case, so the photograph keeps its own colours. It is
also the one place where the size of the file really matters — it is being blown
up to fill a projector, and most of the archive is not big enough. Check
`references/assets.md` before you promise a full-bleed photo of anything.

### `photo-grid`

Three to five photographs arranged as a composition. Fewer than three does not
read as a set; more than five stops reading as a composition and starts reading
as a contact sheet.

This is the **mass** case: the audience reads the group, not any one frame. So
it takes the purple duotone, and so the individual pictures do not have to be
good — a soft 1200px photo of a lit meeting room is a perfectly respectable tile
and a hopeless hero. Choose for what the set says together.

### `stats`

Three or four big numbers with a short label under each, and an optional
photograph beside them. Each number is written display-ready — `"3000+"`,
`"94"`, `"$10k"` — not calculated on screen.

Four is the maximum. A fifth number is not more impressive, it is less.

### `logos`

Sponsor and partner marks, optionally in labelled groups so tiers read clearly
("Principal partners", "Supporting partners"), and at three sizes. Logos sit on
white chips on dark slides, which is what keeps multi-colour marks looking
right — see `references/assets.md`.

### `themes`

Cards, each with a short title, one line of detail, and an optional tag. Up to
six. The detail is one line — the linter rejects two sentences in it.

This is the layout for things the room is going to choose between: hackathon
challenge areas, workshop tracks, panel topics, the three streams of a
conference.

### `criteria`

Named rows, each with a short description and an optional weight. Up to five.
Descriptions are ten words maximum, which is the whole point: a rubric on a
slide is a summary of a rubric, and the real one lives on the event page.

> *Impact — Solves a real problem for real people — 40%*

### `prizes`

Amounts with what each is for, up to four, optionally scoped to distinguish a
venue prize from a national one. A footnote line handles the "terms apply"
pointer without putting the terms on the slide.

### `break`

A dark slide with a countdown clock, sized to be read across a room. **Space
starts and pauses the clock** — on this slide only, Space does not advance.

Set the minutes to what the break actually is. The clock is the most useful
thing the deck does all day: a room comes back on time when it can see the time
running out.

### `qr-cta`

One large code with a label, a human-typable fallback line, and up to three
supporting points. This is the "do this now" slide — give feedback, sign up,
join Slack, submit your project.

One code per slide. Two codes side by side means the room scans neither.

### `contact`

She Sharp's social handles plus two or three codes. It sits near the front, so
someone who arrives curious can follow the organisation before the event has
even started.

Three codes is the hard maximum. A fourth makes all of them too small to scan.

### `upcoming`

Two or three future events, each with a title, a date and optionally a time,
venue, one-line blurb and image, plus a code linking to the events page.

These are **snapshotted when the deck is written**, not looked up live — a live
list would quietly change between the rehearsal and the day.

### `thanks`

The closing thank-you: grouped logos, plus people thanked by name — mentors,
judges, volunteers, the person who ran the door.

Put the names in. It is thirty seconds and it is the reason those people come
back.
